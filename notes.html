On this page

    1. The scenario

    The course describes the scenario in one line: "AI-assisted patient prioritization in a public hospital."
    That means:

    • A hospital has more patients than staff, beds, or time.
    • Someone or something must decide who is seen first.
    • An AI system helps make that decision by ranking or scoring patients.
    • Humans may or may not stay in the loop.

    The ethical question

    • ❌ "Is the AI accurate?"
    • ✅ What trade-offs does each design choice create for fairness, accountability, privacy, sustainability, and staff?

    What we need to build

    • ❌ We are not building a medical tool.
    • ❌ We are not building an actual AI tool. The course does not allow AI-generated judgments to replace our rule-based model.
    • ✅ We are building a model of ethical trade-offs, which covers the decisions a hospital makes about using an AI tool.

    The simulator illustrates trade-offs and does not predict real outcomes.

    The AI tool in this scenario

    The AI is a hypothetical tool. Its intended goal is to:/p>

    • Produce a priority score that staff see.
    • Leave the hospital to decide how much to trust it.

    Some AI-specific ethical issues the group could consider:

    • Bias in the score, where some groups are ranked lower.
    • A black box that nobody can explain.
    • Over-trusting the ranking, also called automation bias.
    • Who is responsible when the AI is wrong.
    • Sensitive health data being fed to the AI.
    • The computing and energy cost of running the AI.

    A public hospital emergency department in the Philippines uses an AI-assisted tool to rank waiting patients.
    • Everyone already understands an ED queue, so the video can explain it in about 30 seconds.
    • A public hospital brings real equity issues: cost, distance, crowding, and limited staff.
    • The Philippine context lets us cite local law (the Data Privacy Act) and DOH policy.
    • It is concrete enough for realistic decisions, and simple enough that no medical expertise is needed.

    Suggested ways to share the work

    Most of the contribution is ideas and evidence. The website only displays what the group decides.

    Five workstreams

    With five members, five decisions, and five indicators, each member can own one slice and review the next member's. Names are not assigned yet, so everyone can pick based on interest. Whoever is most comfortable presenting could take A, since it frames the video.

    Rule cards

    Each rule can be written as a rule card, one per rule, either in a shared doc or directly in the Excel table (rules.xlsx):

    Rule ID:            R-D4-02
IF (decision):      Transparency = Black box
THEN (indicator):   Accountability   -15
BECAUSE:            Nobody can inspect or contest an unexplained ranking.
Source:             WHO 2021 guidance, section on transparency (verify link)
How weight was set: Large effect (-15) because it removes the ability to appeal.
Course concept:     Responsible innovation, accountability
Assumption:         Staff have no other way to see the score logic.
Written by:         <name>      Reviewed by: <name>

    A rule card covers what the grader looks for on the How the Model Works page: what the rule does, the evidence, how the weight was set, the course concept, and any assumption. The numbers then go into js/model-config.js and the justification into assets/tables/rules.xlsx. The README section Edit and upload table files explains how to edit the Excel tables.

    Shared responsibilities

    Along the way, each member can:

    • Write rule cards for their decision and indicator (about 3 to 6 cards each).
    • Review one other member's cards.
    • Add their references to the References page.
    • Present their part in the video.
    • Record their work in the "Who did what" table.

    Video outline

    A 10 to 12 minute video, with every member on camera, in this order:

    1. Scenario and why it matters (workstream A).
    2. Model walkthrough: variables, indicators, key rules, and one interaction (B, C, and D split this).
    3. Demo: how to use the site, then two contrasting decision sets and how the outcomes differ (E).
    4. Ethical insights and limitations: who gains, who loses, and what the model cannot capture (everyone, one sentence each).

    How decisions could be made

    A light process can keep the group moving:

    1. The owner proposes. The member who owns a decision writes its options and rules.
    2. A 48-hour window. The group can respond in the chat, and silence counts as agreement.
    3. Disagreements. A short 15-minute call. If the group is still stuck, the team leader decides.
    4. A scope freeze. Once the group agrees on the scope, later changes only fix errors instead of adding ideas.
    5. One shared document is the source of truth. The chat is for talking, and the document is for decisions.

    7. What is graded

    The website is the easier part. Ethical reasoning and model quality carry 60% of the grade, so ideas and evidence are the most valuable contribution.

    Requirements to keep in view

    • At least 5 decisions, each with 2 or more real options.
    • At least 5 indicators, covering equity, governance, and sustainability, plus others.
    • At least 4 interactions, each on a different pair of decisions, each justified.
    • Apply at least 2 of: ethical frameworks, privacy and data rights, cybersecurity, AI, and software development. The scenario is AI-assisted, so AI is covered by default. Privacy and cybersecurity are good additions.
    • Everything (rules, references, explanations) lives inside the website, with no separate report.
    • Every member contributes and appears in the video.
